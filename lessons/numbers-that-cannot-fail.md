# The numbers that cannot fail

*Shareable write-up · 2026-08-17 · drawn from a large sub-national geospatial panel —
thousands of areal units, several survey waves, a few hundred variables, plus a generated
stakeholder handoff (a slide deck and a spreadsheet data dictionary). The study is unpublished
and is not identified here; every lesson is about **process**, and none needs the findings.
Where a research statistic is unavoidable it appears as a magnitude only.*

**A number typed into source code cannot fail.** It has nothing to disagree with, so no test,
no gate and no reviewer can detect that it is wrong. Four defects here shared that single
mechanism, they concealed each other, and every automated check in the repository was green
before and after.

---

## The setup

A generated deck and data dictionary described the panel for a downstream researcher. The
generator's own docstring opened with a promise:

> Build the handoff presentation from measured facts, never from typed-in numbers. Every
> figure on every slide is read from a facts file, so the deck cannot drift from the panel.

The deck's title slide repeated it to the reader: *"Every figure in this deck is measured from
the shipped panel, not quoted from notes."*

Both statements were false, and had been for months. Worse, **the promise is what made the
remaining literals invisible** — a reader who believes the numbers are measured does not
check them, and I was that reader.

There was even a warning in the file's own history: a previous fix had removed *"three
hardcoded figures [that] went stale when the frame changed."* That fix removed three
instances. It did not remove the class. **Four more survived.**

---

## The four defects

### 1. A document contradicting itself by 141 units

One slide printed the distribution of survey waves per unit, read correctly from the facts
file. Six slides later, a recommendation read *"run the outcome on the **3,993** three-wave
units."* The measured figure was **4,134**.

The two numbers were the same quantity, in the same document, 141 apart.

**Why the screen could not catch it.** A review script existed specifically to compare every
number on every slide against the facts file. It reported *"No layout or accuracy problems
detected"* — before and after the fix. It was not broken: **a bare literal in prose has no
fact to compare against.** The checker could only verify the numbers that were already wired
correctly.

**How it was found.** By rendering the deck to images and reading one slide against another.
The script's own docstring insisted on exactly this — *"the screen is not the review… then
LOOK at the PNGs"* — and it had been skipped for 29 of 32 slides.

**Guard:** a number-checking gate proves the *wired* numbers are right. It says nothing about
the unwired ones, and its clean report reads as if it does. Grep the generator for numeric
literals in strings; that list is your actual audit surface.

---

### 2. A typed lookup table, missing three keys

The panel's four-region grouping was a hand-written list of 2-digit parent-area codes:

```python
REGIONS = {
    "North": [50, 51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 63, 64, 65, 66, 67],
    ...
}
```

`63` through `67` are present. **`60`, `61`, `62` are not** — and they were in no other region
either. Three real parent areas, **277 units, 3.7% of the frame**, mapped to null.

**Why nothing failed.** The map is applied with `.map()`, which yields null for an unknown key,
and every consumer is a `groupby` or `crosstab`, which **silently discards nulls**. The output
kept its exact shape — four regions, same columns, no warning. Losing 3.7% of the frame
presented as a regional median shifting in its fourth decimal place.

**Why the list looked plausible.** The identifier space has real gaps. Three of the four
region lists were written as contiguous runs whose gaps are *dead codes* — identifiers that do
not exist. Only one list's gap contained live ones:

| list | gap | codes skipped | real areas in the gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| South | 87–89 | 87, 88, 89 | **none** — dead codes |
| North | 59–62 | 59, 60, 61, 62 | **60, 61, 62** ← three real |
| Northeast | — | generated by `range()` | n/a |

*List the runs, skip the gaps* was correct three times by luck of the identifier space, and
wrong once. The one list that could not be wrong was the one generated by code.

**The part that stings.** A committed workbook in the same repository carried a region column
for all parent areas. Its provenance was already logged in a research note. **Another script
already read that same workbook** for a different purpose. The literal was a duplicate of a
table that already existed, and it disagreed with it.

**Cost:** every regional statistic in the analysis — a period × region crosstab, per-region
medians, and a rank test across regions — all of it shipped on two slides of the handoff.

---

### 3. A statistic that could not move when its own population was wrong

The rank test comparing regions had its test statistic and p-value **typed as literals into
the facts dictionary**, and the figure title was a hardcoded string asserting a specific
effect ratio.

So when defect 2 removed 3.7% of the population from that very test, the statistic reporting
it **did not budge** — it could not. Recomputed on the complete population, the test statistic
shifted by ~6%, the p-value by five orders of magnitude, and the headline ratio had been
understated by ~10%.

**Two typed values covering for each other.** The population was wrong, and the only number
that would have revealed it was itself a literal. Either defect alone would have been
detectable; together they were airtight.

**Guard:** the statistic most worth computing is the one that would expose a defect elsewhere.
If it is typed, you have disabled your own smoke detector.

---

### 4. The fix that was the same defect

On finding three codes missing, I added three codes to the list. I also wrote a comment
asserting this *"completes the standard 17-area Northern region"* — **a figure I recalled
rather than verified.**

In the same commit message I criticised the earlier fix for *"removing the instances but not
the class."*

It happened to be correct: checked afterwards against the workbook, the corrected map matched
on all four regions. **It was right by luck, not by evidence** — and it left the literal in
place for the next person to get wrong.

The real fix deleted the literal. A small module reads the workbook, asserts the expected area
count, no duplicate keys, no unmapped category value, and that the regions **partition** the
population; it exposes an `assert_covers()` guard called with the *consuming* dataset's keys;
it has **no fallback literal**, because a fallback is exactly how a stale hand-written copy
survives; and its self-test **replays the original defect** and proves the guard raises, rather
than only proving it passes. Verified behaviour-preserving: byte-identical output.

---

## How they were actually found

Not by any check. Four separate gates were green throughout — the deck's number screen, a
pipeline completeness audit, a terrain validator, a board consistency checker. Two acts found
everything:

1. **Rendering the artifact and reading it.** Defect 1 came from comparing two slides of the
   same document by eye.
2. **Recomputing a stated number from source instead of reading it.** Defects 2 and 3 came out
   of an unrelated verification task that required rebuilding the classifier — and therefore
   counting rows. Reading the stated statistic would have found nothing.

The trigger for both was a question from outside: *had the figures actually been looked at,*
and *does every group carry the right data.* Neither was a question the checks could ask
themselves.

---

## The asymmetry worth keeping

**A wrong entry announces itself; a missing entry does not.** A misassigned key produces a
visibly odd answer. An absent key produces a *slightly different* answer, which is
indistinguishable from the truth unless you count.

That is why the guard has to be on the **population**, not on the table. Asserting the table
is internally consistent proves nothing: every row in it mapped fine. The check that fires is
*"does every key in the data I am about to classify have a class?"*

**And a promise of correctness is not a mechanism.** The docstring saying "never from typed-in
numbers" made the literals *harder* to find, because it earned the trust that suppressed the
check. If a claim like that is worth making, it needs a test that fails when it stops being
true.

---

## What to actually do

- **Grep your generators for numeric literals inside strings.** That set is the audit surface
  a number-checking gate cannot see.
- **Never type reference data.** Find the authority — it is often already committed, and
  already read by another script. Read it, assert its shape, and refuse any fallback.
- **Assert the classifier covers the population**, using the consuming dataset's own keys.
- **Compute the statistic that would expose your other bugs.** A typed one is a disabled alarm.
- **Render the artifact and read it**, then say exactly what was eyeballed and what was only
  screened. "I reviewed them" when you screened them is a false claim about your own work.
- **When you find a typed value that is wrong, delete the literal — do not correct it.**
  Correcting it is the same defect with a later expiry date.
